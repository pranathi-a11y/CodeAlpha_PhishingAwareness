01 / KNOW THE PATTERNS

Phishing has a pattern.
Learn to recognise it.

Phishing is a deceptive attempt to make someone reveal information, open a harmful file, send money, or take another action that benefits an attacker.

✉

01 — THE MESSAGE

Phishing emails & messages

Unsolicited messages can imitate services you use and steer you toward a link, attachment, or request.

Spot itUnexpected requests, odd sender addresses, mismatched link destinations, or requests for secrets.
⌁

02 — THE DESTINATION

Fake sites & lookalike domains

A convincing page can still be controlled by an attacker. A familiar logo is not proof of legitimacy.

Spot itMisspellings, extra words, unexpected subdomains, or a domain that differs from the official one.
◉

03 — THE PRESSURE

Social engineering

Attackers exploit trust and emotion—posing as colleagues, support staff, delivery services, or authority figures.

Spot itA request that bypasses normal process, secrecy, or pressure to act without checking.
!

04 — THE DEADLINE

Urgency, fear & curiosity

“Act now,” “your account will close,” or a surprise reward can be designed to shut down careful thinking.

Spot itThreats or prizes paired with an immediate click, payment, or login request.
↳

05 — THE FILE

Suspicious links & attachments

Unexpected files or links may lead to deceptive pages or attempt to run harmful content.

Spot itAn unexpected document, unusual file type, or request to enable content or macros.
✓

YOUR SAFER DEFAULT

Pause. Check. Verify.

Do not reply or use contact details supplied in the suspicious message. Open the official app or type a known address yourself, then verify through a trusted channel.

02 / TRAIN YOUR EYE

Looks familiar.
That’s the trap.

These fictional training scenarios show how a message can look ordinary while using pressure or deception. No links are live.

SIMULATION 01ACCOUNT ALERT
◈ Account Desk FICTIONAL

Immediate action required

From: “Account Security” Unverified sender

Your account will be restricted today. Confirm your details immediately to keep access.

Illustrative button — not clickable

Training simulation — not a real service.

What should make you pause?

  • 01
    Artificial urgency

    A same-day threat pressures you to act before thinking.

  • 02
    Vague identity

    The sender’s display name does not establish who sent it.

  • 03
    Request to “confirm details”

    Unexpected account requests should be checked independently.

Safer response

Do not use the message button. Open the official service independently and check account notifications.

SIMULATION 02DELIVERY NOTICE
▣ Parcel Update FICTIONAL

Delivery could not be completed

From: “Shipment Team” Not verified

A small fee is required to reschedule your delivery. Complete payment now or the parcel may be returned.

Illustrative button — not clickable

Training simulation — no payment link is provided.

What should make you pause?

  • 01
    Unexpected fee

    Attackers may invent minor charges to solicit payment details.

  • 02
    Threat of losing a parcel

    Fear and a deadline make a quick response feel necessary.

  • 03
    No independent confirmation

    A convincing delivery story is not evidence the request is genuine.

Safer response

Check the order in the retailer’s official app or visit the carrier’s known official website independently.

SIMULATION 03IMPERSONATION
▤ Workplace Message FICTIONAL

Confidential — need this now

From: “Department Lead” Identity unverified

I’m in a meeting. Buy gift cards and send me the codes now. Please keep this between us.

Request is unverified

Training simulation — no real person is represented.

What should make you pause?

  • 01
    Authority and urgency

    The sender expects compliance without normal review.

  • 02
    Unusual payment method

    Gift-card codes are difficult to recover once shared.

  • 03
    Secrecy request

    Being told not to verify is a strong warning sign.

Safer response

Do not purchase or send anything. Contact the person through a known number or approved workplace channel and follow payment procedures.

ⓘ All three messages above are fictional educational simulations. They do not represent real organisations or live threats.

03 / LEARN FROM REAL INCIDENTS

Real tactics.
Real consequences.

These historical cases show how social engineering can reach beyond a single inbox. Read the source material for full context.

CASE FILE 012015
✉!

BUSINESS EMAIL COMPROMISE

Ubiquiti Networks wire-transfer fraud

In its 2016 annual SEC filing (Form 10-K), Ubiquiti Networks reported that it had been a victim of business email compromise involving employee impersonation and fraudulent requests aimed at its finance department. The company reported transfers totalling $46.7 million to overseas accounts.

Technique

Impersonation and fraudulent transfer requests exploited trust in business communication and financial processes.

What defenders can learn

Independently confirm transfer instructions using a trusted channel, require dual approval, and treat unusual secrecy or urgency as a reason to slow down.

Read the company’s SEC filing ↗

Source: Ubiquiti Networks, Form 10-K covering fiscal year 2016, filed with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. The filing describes the June 2015 fraud.

CASE FILE 022020
◎↗

PHONE SPEAR PHISHING & IMPERSONATION

Twitter’s July 2020 social-engineering attack

Twitter reported that attackers targeted employees through phone spear phishing and gained access to internal systems and support tools. The attackers targeted 130 accounts, tweeted from 45, accessed direct-message inboxes for 36, and downloaded account data for 7, according to the company’s July 30 update.

Technique

Targeted phone-based social engineering, followed by misuse of employee credentials and internal account-support tools.

What defenders can learn

Verify unexpected internal requests, restrict privileged access, train employees to report suspicious approaches, and monitor the use of sensitive tools.

Read Twitter’s incident update ↗

Source: Twitter, “An update on our security incident,” originally published July 18 and updated July 30, 2020. The company described a phone spear-phishing attack.

i

Keep the context: these cases use social engineering and impersonation in different ways. Not every social-engineering incident is a conventional phishing email. The shared lesson is to verify identity and unusual requests through an independent, trusted route.

04 / VERIFY BEFORE YOU TRUST

Good security starts
with one extra check.

A realistic logo and a secure connection can create confidence. Use several signals and verify unexpected requests independently.

01
⌁

Read the whole domain

Look for misspellings, added words, unexpected subdomains, and domains that imitate a trusted brand. A familiar word elsewhere in the address is not enough.

02
⌑

HTTPS is not a trust badge

HTTPS encrypts a connection to a site. It does not prove the site is honest or belongs to the organisation it claims to represent.

PADLOCK ≠ PROOF OF LEGITIMACY
03
↗

Do not trust a surprise login

Unexpected sign-in prompts can be a lure. Avoid the message link; open the service using your saved bookmark or a known address.

04
◎

Verify out of band

Contact the sender with a known phone number or approved chat—not the contact details included in the suspicious message.

05
⌕

Inspect without opening

On a computer, you may inspect a link destination without clicking. Do not visit suspicious destinations just to investigate them. Follow your organisation’s reporting process.

06
▣

Protect passwords and codes

Never share a password or one-time verification code in response to an unexpected request. Use a password manager and phishing-resistant sign-in methods where available.

06 / PREPARE, DON'T PANIC

Make safe habits
your default.

A few repeatable habits reduce risk. If something goes wrong, respond promptly and proportionately rather than assuming the worst.

✓

IF SOMETHING HAPPENS

Respond in the right order.

01
You clicked a link

Close the page. Do not enter information or approve prompts. A click alone does not prove compromise. If a file downloaded, an alert appeared, or something seems wrong, contact your IT/security team or a trusted technician.

02
You shared a password or code

From the genuine service or app, change the exposed password promptly, change it anywhere it was reused, revoke active sessions where possible, and review MFA and account activity. Notify your IT/security team if it is a work account.

03
You shared payment or banking details

Contact your bank or payment provider using the number or app you already trust. Explain what was exposed and follow their advice. Report fraudulent transactions promptly.

04
Keep the evidence and report

Use your organisation’s approved reporting process. Preserve the message and relevant time/details if safe to do so; do not forward suspicious content widely.

!

Work device or account? Contact your IT/security team promptly and follow its incident-response instructions.
